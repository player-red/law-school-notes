Chapters 3, 5 and 9

Chapter 3: Early Rome to 500 BCE

- Rome's Monarchy/Regal Period ended in 500 BCE.
- Marcus Terentius Varro (antiquarian) believes Rome was founded on 21st April, 753 BCE. 

The Ancient Literary Tradition and Its Sources
- Oldest literary accounts from 50-0 BCE.
	- Cicero: De Re Publica (50 BCE)
	- Livy: Ab Urbe Condita, Book One (~25 BCE)
- Greek writers who wrote about Rome's beginnings:
	- Dionysius of Halicarnassus: Roman Antiquities 
	- Diodorus Siculus: Books 7-9 of his world history
	- Plutarch: biographer and essayist.
	- Cassius Dio: Roman History, 2nd-3rd centuries CE.
Sources of the literary tradition
- Ancient records (inscriptions on stones, monuments, oral traditions) especially from Latin, Etruscan and Greek neighbors.

Deconstruction of Rome's founding myth (story of Romulus and Remus)
- Greek settlers in Italy wanted to link their new settlement to the legends of Greece.
- Latinus (son of Odysseus and Circe) was the king of Etruscans (the Greeks did not distinguish between Etruscans and Latins). 
  Latinus = Latium, Latin people
- Aeneas journeyed to Italy where he was associated with the Etruscans, who were the new rivals of the Greeks. 
- Ancient depictions of the she-wolf who suckled Romulus and Remus — 400 and 340 BCE. Possibly influenced by the Etruscans.
- Alban origin of Romulus and Remus: possibly Alban propaganda to claim control over Rome. 
- The site at which Rome was established was inhabited long before 753 BCE. Romulus and Remus were likely not historical figures. 
- The fall of Troy was set to be in 1184 BCE — Aeneas could not have wandered for 370~ years till the founding of Rome. Hence, a list of kings of Alba Longa was inserted to explain the gap, till Romulus' grandfather Numitor.

The rise of Greek city-states and its impact on Rome
- Rome's founding by one specific man was likely the result of Classical and Hellenistic Greek historians trying to connect Rome to their past.
- Rome was no different from the early Greek city states.
- Archeological evidence shows that Rome was produced by a large, unconscious process that produced many city-states across the world. 
- The development of Rome was similar to the Greek poleis but it soon outgrew this system and became a dominant state in the West.

Early Rome and Latium
- Rome had been continuously inhabited from 1200 to 1000 BCE but urbanization began in the 8th Century BCE. 
- Before urbanization bean, Indo-European villagers lived as farmers and herders. This was reflected in Roman legends and language — Remus was said to be brought up in a shepherd's cottage.
- Pastoral tradition led to many animal sacrifices to the gods.
- Evolution of language: words that were originally related to pastoral things such as flocks of animals later evolved to have more abstract meaning. 

1000 to 700 BCE
- Earliest graves were cremation burials.
- 830 BCE: inhumation burials with some objects such as vases and jewelry buried, fibula, spindle whorls and looms, etc.
- Later, people were buried with weapons but not during this period.

The seventh century BCE
- Graves became more luxurious, with corpses being adorned with gold, silver and bronze, weapons and chariots.
- Houses became more substantial with roofs of heavy terracotta supported by wooden beams.
- The pomerium: boundary between civil and military spheres.

The Early Roman State
- Rome came into existence around 625 BCE, when the Forum was paved and began to have shrines and temples.
- Rome never had a written constitution: used custom, precedent and legislation. 

The kings
- A cup with the word "Rex" on it was excavated at the Regia: clear proof that Rome was ruled by kings.
- After the monarchy, the pontifex maximus occupied the Regia, next to the Temple of Vesta.
- Terms and titles related to kings were also used during the Republic. 

Nature of early Roman kingship
- More religious than absolute or hereditary. 
- Rex sacrorum: gods who guaranteed welfare of Rome wanted a king to serve.
- Interrex appointed occasionally.
- It later became important for the appointment of a king to be ratified by arms-bearing men (comitia curiata).
- Kings negotiated treaties, make war etc since he was the leading religious and military authority, had the final word on public affairs.
- Did not function alone and sought the advice of others.

The senate
- The senate became the State's most powerful institution during the Republic.
- Advisory body of elders that would have advised kings in monarchy.
- Gave advice only when summoned by the king during the Republic and he could ignore their advice and risk enmity of many powerful men.

The army and the earliest popular assembly, the comitia curiata
- The arms-bearing adult male citizens were called the populus.
- They formed both the army and the Curiate Assembly.
- Originally, citizens were divided into three tribes (Ramnes, Tities, Luceres) and represented a major district of the city.
	- Each tribe subdivided into smaller districts called curiae.
	- 30 curiae in total.
- Theoretically had sovereign power and inaugurated the new king. 
- Generally showed approval and disapproval of the king's policies, and the king generally had to seek their approval in order to command military power efficiently.

The evolution of the army and the comitia centuriata (new popular assembly)
- Formation of formal Roman state led to spread of new weapons, armor, military tactics, etc.
- Hoplite phalanx was a technique used by the Greek army and was almost indestructible if every man kept formation. 
- The army was formed of a selection of able-bodied men.
- Eventually the entire adult male citizen body was organized into 193 centuries (groups of 100 men) and ranked by the value of their property and the kind of services they could provide.
- In the early Republic, they became the popular assembly, the comitia centuriata.

Chapter 5 — From tyrant kings to oligarchic republic, 509 to 287 BCE.
- 'Republic' is derived from 'res publica' meaning common wealth or public thing.
- People may play a role in governance through election, but it is not necessarily a democracy.
- Meant opposition one-man rule for the Romans after being ruled by an emperor who had almost unlimited control over everything.
- Senatorial class was limited to the elites, but they governed using law and institutions to prevent arbitrariness.

The Fasti
- Inscriptions that preserve the list of annual consuls.
- Years were not numbered in sequence, but named after the annual officials.
- Originated in the annual records kept by the pontifex maximus.
- All versions of the Fasti are probably based on a common stock of source material that was preserved due to tradition. 

From Kingship to Republic 
- 550 BCE: Rome became a large and complex site with a large territory. Other Greek cities had fallen under the rule of 'popular tyrants'.
	- Rome's last kings probably acted in a similar way, using the army to pursue expansion.
The final struggles
- Violent end of the monarchy at the end of the sixth or the beginning of the 5th century BCE. 
- 500 BCE: Regia and the Comitium were burned down.

The Early Form of the Republic
- Roots were in the institutions established by the monarchy, but the aristocrats from the elite patrician families were the ones in power.
- Created a system to keep themselves in control and prevent any one aristocrat from gaining too much power.

The consulship 
- Chief magistrates: chief commander, commander of cavalry/civil judge.
- Each had the power to veto the other, preventing the concentration of power.
- Acted as judges, summoned meetings of the comitia, and placed legislative proposals before it.

The office of dictator
- A magistrate with imperium appointed a dictator in times of crisis.
- Maximum period: six months.
- Consuls remained in office but became his subordinates.
- Had 24 lictors to attend to him.

The senator
- The function of the senate was the same as it was in the monarchy, ie being an advisory council.
- Only officially met at the summons of a consul or other imperium holder, praetor, etc.
- The decree of the senate was not legally binding since the senate could not pass laws.
- A magistrate could legally enforce a decree, but with the power to change or reject it completely.
- Controlled foreign affairs, expenditure, and their decrees were rarely ever overridden by lawmakers.
- Meetings had to take place in a public consecrated place to ensure that they had the approval of divinity. 
- Eventually became a major organ of government that dominated the state due to the aristocrats who were a part of it. 

The comitia centuriata and comitia curiata
- Women never had the right to vote or hold political office, but all male Roman citizens were members of all assemblies.

Priesthoods and Priestly Colleges
- Used to maintain Rome's relationship with the gods.
- Pontiffs, augurs, fetials, duvoirs.
- Women chosen because they were wives of priests and could not divorce, and other women chosen because they had never been married. 
- Vestal Virgins came from the most prestigious families and should not have had physical defects.
- Qualification for priesthood was pedigree, not piety.
