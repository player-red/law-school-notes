By Patricia Uberoi, [[Y1 T1 SOC Society/PEOPLE/Satish Deshpande\|Satish Deshpande]] and Nandini Sundar, chapter 1: The Professionalization of Indian Anthropology and Sociology: People, Places, and Institutions.

I. Disciplinary History in India: Its Potential Importance
- Disciplinary history is hard to preserve in India, due to the lack of proper mechanisms.
- Combining sociology and social + cultural anthropology.
- Anthropology generally thought to study "other" cultures in the West, such as tribal, primitive, pre-modern societies, whereas sociology is thought of as the study of modern industrial societies. ([[Y1 T1 SOC Society/CONCEPTS/Othering\|Othering]])

II. Colonial Domination and the Colonised Mind
- Knowledge of 'primitive others' was not only facilitated through anthropological study, but also colonial rule & domination.
- Asad argues that anthropology is rooted in an unequal power relationship between the West and the Third World.
- Anthropological knowledge of a tradition distilled it and froze it in time, ignoring the changing reality of society. ([[Y2 T1 SOC Social Forces (MC I)/PEOPLE/Edward Said\|Edward Said]], Orientalism. & [[Y2 T1 SOC Social Forces (MC I)/READINGS/SOC3.01 Cohn, The Census, Social Structure and Objectification in South Asia\|SOC3.01 Cohn, The Census, Social Structure and Objectification in South Asia]])
- Focused on small units rather than the relationships between these units and economic and political systems.
- Neglected history and the impact of colonial policy.
- Fieldwork criticized: asymmetry between 'observer' and 'observed', inherent bias.
- Imposition of Western methods of teaching and thought, colonised the non-Western mind and led to formerly colonised people inheriting academic colonialism.
- Patrick Geddes: saw India as an experimental laboratory to test an alternative to Western modernisation.
- [[Y2 T1 SOC Social Forces (MC I)/PEOPLE/Verrier Elwin\|Verrier Elwin]] celebrated indigenous adivasi traditions and criticised both colonialism and Hindu nationalism.
- Ram argues that even some Indians themselves took on the role of ethnographer, due to their propensity to the civilising mission of Westerners (such as Tamil Brahmans).
- Roy argued that Indian anthropologists could do better than foreigners in studying cultures of Indian tribal people, and that modern science could be reconciled with the ideas in the Vedas.
- Indian pioneers saw themselves as studying their own society, equal citizens of India, often wrote bilingually.
- Translating traditional texts into modern language.

III. The Uses of Biography in Disciplinary History
- Biography: 'space in which there is an interaction between lived and written texts'.
- Knowledge production is a social process mediated by and through individuals. (Max Gluckman)
- For example, reading private diaries alongside published ethnographic reports reveals hidden details that shaped anthropology. 
- Hindu middle-class life and the influence it exerted on professional and intellectual life: stayed connected with tradition while engaging in modern scientific practices.
- Habitus ([[Y1 T1 SOC Society/PEOPLE/Pierre Bourdieu\|Pierre Bourdieu]]) of the biographer themselves is an active component in the production of the biography. 
- Indian sociological sensibility has evidence to support its development over time.

IV. Themes and Issues
- Could be said to fulfill the British ideal of 'Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, opinions, morals & intellect'.
- Two challenges to native practitioners of socio/anthro:
	- Surpassing colonial science by mimicking its methods but using status as a native + local to their advantage (Iyer and Roy).
	- Explore local traditions to show the prior presence of Indian tradition. 
- Anthropology traditionally + administratively classified as an extension of the biological sciences. 
- The pursuit of science requires specialization, creating exclusion and esotericism which defeated the point of cultural regeneration. Many sociologists ended up writing popular literature freed from constraints imposed by scientific writing.
(B) Disciplinary Boundaries
- Creation of boundaries demarcating what was sociology and anthropology from the 1920s to 1940s in order to define the disciplines.
- Sanskrit learning was easily incorporated.
- Combining sociology with other disciplines such as archeology, town planning, civics, etc. in universities.
(C) Nationalism and the Nation-State
- Indian anthropologists/sociologists did not oppose nationalism, unless it was bigoted.
- Saw India as a plural society, not just one of sanskritic or brahmanical Hinduism. 
- Wanted to protect and preserve the cultures of the tribes from exploitation by non-tribal people. 
- Helped formulate government development projects.
(D) Questions of Methodology
- Verrier Elwin: proponent of tribal isolationism, Geddes: Scotsman who believed local knowledge should be used for town planning.
- Concern about nationalist desire to include all people within India despite differences in development and culture.
(E) Sociology/Anthropology — For Whom?
- For policymakers, academics and international recognition.
- For the general reader (especially articles written in vernacular languages).
(F) The Study of 'Other' Societies
- Indian diaspora often return to India to study South Asian studies and conduct fieldwork as 'native informants'.
- Ambition for comparative understanding of Indian society (by comparing to both Western and non-Western societies).

V. Disciplinary Memory
- Rarely study societies outside of India in order to develop a comparative tradition. 
- Many of the forefathers of Indian sociology have been forgotten.
