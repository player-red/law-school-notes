Chapter 1 in Sociological Theory, 32-79.

Abdel Rahman Ibn-Khaldun
- Born in Tunis, North Africa, in 1332.
- Believed that state rulers were not divine rulers.
- Believed in the importance of linking sociology and history.
- "Asabiyyah" (group cohesiveness): concept of social solidarity which refers to unity, group consciousness, social cohesion and shared purpose.
	- Used this concept to study what he believed was the cyclical nature of state formation. 
	- Defined two types of groups in North Africa, the pastoral nomadic people and sedentary societies.
	- Stated that since pastoral nomadic people had high levels of asabiyyah, they were able to subdue sedentary societies and establish their rule.
	- Once they settled down after subjugation, their level of asabiyyah would decrease and they would be defeated by other pastoral nomadic groups with higher asabiyyah.

Political Revolutions
- The result of the political revolutions that came after the 1789 French Revolution was renewed interest in sociological theorizing. 
- Finding new bases of order in societies.

The Industrial Revolution and the Rise of Capitalism
- Changed the Western world from an agricultural to an industrial society.
- [[Y1 T1 SOC Society/PEOPLE/Karl Marx\|Karl Marx]], [[Y2 T1 SOC Social Forces (MC I)/PEOPLE/Max Weber\|Max Weber]], [[Y1 T1 SOC Society/PEOPLE/Émile Durkheim\|Émile Durkheim]], etc. were thinkers who studied the effects of capitalism and the problems it created for society.

Colonialism
- Marx explains how capitalism led to the push for colonization.
- Europeans, in attempting to rationalize colonialism, developed modern racism and Social Darwinism. 

The Rise of Socialism
- Most early theorists (except Marx) opposed socialism and instead advocated for social reform within capitalism.

Feminism
- Men who were recognized as central to sociology responded to feminist critiques in a conventional way and dismissed the idea of gender being central to the study of society.

Urbanization, Religious Change, the Growth of Science.

Intellectual Forces and the Rise of Sociological Theory
The Enlightenment
- 'Early sociology developed as a reaction to the Enlightenment.'
- 17th century philosophy & science as main inspiration:
	- Early thinkers: Creating large-scale systems of general ideas that made rational sense.
	- Later thinkers: more emphasis on real world application, combining empirical research and reason, based on Newtonian physics.
	- Rejected traditional beliefs grounded in irrationality.
Conservative Reaction to the Enlightenment
- Counter-enlightenment beliefs along with enlightenment ideology influenced French sociology.
- Conservative beliefs recognized the importance of irrational elements in social life (imagination, tradition, religion) and gave them positive value. 
- Study of the society as something more than a collection of individuals.

Development of French Sociology
Alexis de Tocqueville 
- Advocate for freedom, warning against centralization which restricted freedom.
- Argued that equality led to "mediocrity" and a servile people who would not oppose centralization. He believed the aristocrats were the class who could keep centralization in check. 
- Linked equality to individualism and stated that they were not interested in the well-being of the community, unlike aristocrats.

Claude Henri Saint-Simon
- Advocated for both conservatism and positivism, believing that society had to be studied using scientific techniques.
- Saw the need for socialist reforms but did not see the working class replacing feudal nobility.

Auguste Comte
- Developed positivism to combat Enlightenment thinking.
- Law of the three stages: theological -> metaphysical -> positivistic 
- Positivism would stop social revolution. 
- Basic unit of analysis was larger than the individual, for example like the family. 

[[Y1 T1 SOC Society/PEOPLE/Émile Durkheim\|Émile Durkheim]]
- Legitimized sociology in France.
- Feared and hated social disorder, thought them unnecessary and that they could be reduced by social reforms. 
- Sociology as the study of social facts: forces that are external to and coercive of the individual.
	- Material (bureaucracy and law) and nonmaterial (cultural and social institutions).
- Religion: ([[Y1 T1 SOC Society/READINGS/PRIMARY/SOC8.26 Durkheim, The Elementary Forms of Religious Life\|SOC8.26 Durkheim, The Elementary Forms of Religious Life]]) the source of religion as society itself.
	- Some things defined as religious and profane.

The Development of German Sociology
The Roots and Nature of the Theories of Karl Marx
Hegel
- Dialectic (dynamic way of viewing the world through processes, relationships, conflicts, etc) and idealism (importance of the mind and mental products over the material world).
- Created a general theory of evolution of the world, where change occurs at the level of consciousness.
Feuerbach
- Bridge between Hegel and Marx.
- More materialist philosophy, moving away from Hegel's focus on consciousness.
- Focused on religion and how it reflected human nature, and how the concept of god alienated humans from their positive aspects.
Marx, Hegel and Feuerbach
- Marx pushed back against Hegel's idealist philosophy, believing that it led to a conservative orientation.
- He argued that problems of modern life had real sources and the solution would be to overturn those structures through collective action.
- Marx combined Hegel's dialectic and Feuerbach's materialism to create dialectical materialism.
Political Economy
- Labour theory of value: the capitalist profits from the exploitation of the labourer.
- Did not passively accept capitalism and encourage people to find success within it.
Marx and Sociology
- Not considered a sociologist, and his radical work was feared by classical sociologists who were conservative as a reaction to the Enlightenment and the French Revolution. 
Marx's Theory
- People are basically productive and need to work in & with nature to survive, and they are inherently social. 
- Capitalism is a series of structures that creates barriers between an individual and the production process, leading to alienation. ([[ARCHIVE/Y1/Y1 T2/Y1 T2 Power/READINGS/POW9.01 Marx, Estranged Labour\|POW9.01 Marx, Estranged Labour]])

The Roots and Nature of the Theories of Max Weber and Georg Simmel
Weber and Marx
- Marxian theory was a negative in Weberian theory.
- Saw Marxists as determinists who had single-cause theories of social life. (Not true for Marx himself, but later Marxists)
- Studied the effect of ideas on the economy, such as the Protestant Work Ethic.
- Extended Marx's ideas by studying stratification based on status and power, not only social class.
Other Influences on Weber
- Immanuel Kant (the world as something that can never be known directly, only through thought processes that filter it) and Friedrich Nietzsche (an individual who stands up to bureaucracy and other modern societal structures).

Weber's Theory
- Theory of [[Y2 T1 SOC Social Forces (MC I)/CONCEPTS/Rationalization\|Rationalization]].
- Types of authority systems: traditional, charismatic, rational-legal.
- Theory accepted because it was more politically acceptable than Marxism, and did not propose radical solutions.

Simmel's Theory
- Small scale issues unlike Weber and Marx.
- Kantian philosophy — forms of interactions and types of interactants could be classified into a limited number.
- Viewed human action as an expression of ever-changing, dynamic life forces. 
- Thought the importance of the individual decreased the more the culture and its components expanded.

The Origins of British Sociology
Sigmund Freud
- Treated hysteria by letting the patient talk about it.
- Saw libido as the root of neuroses.
- Developed the field of psychoanalysis.
Political Economy, Ameliorism, and Social Evolution
Political Economy
- British sociologists tended to accept [[ARCHIVE/Y1/Y1 T2/Y1 T2 Economy/PEOPLE/Adam Smith\|Adam Smith]]'s idea of the [[ARCHIVE/Y1/Y1 T2/Y1 T2 Economy/CONCEPTS/Invisible Hand\|Invisible Hand]].
- Saw the market as a positive force that allowed the integration of society, unlike Marx.
Ameliorism
- Wanted to reform societal problems by reforming individuals.
- Wanted to prevent socialist society, conservatively oriented.
Social evolution
- It is the gradual development of organized societal structures due to adaptation.

Herbert Spencer
Spencer and Comte
- Seen as a political liberal who became more conservative.
- Accepted a laissez-faire doctrine and not interested in social reform. 
- He was a social Darwinist who believed that the world should be left alone and would eventually evolve to become ideal. 
- Saw the social world as an organism, much like Comte and Durkheim. 
- Critical of Comte's theory of evolution and rejected the three stages wanted evolution in the material world.
Evolutionary Theory
- Society grows in 2 ways:
	- Union of groups
	- Multiplication of individuals
- Movement of society from simple to compound, to doubly and trebly compound. 
The Reaction Against Spencer in Britain
- His idea of the survival of the fittest was a threat to the proponents of ameliorism. 

Vilfredo Pareto
- Developed his ideas to refute Marx and other Enlightenment thinkers.
- Theory of social change: based around a small group of elites — when these elites degenerate and are replaced by the nongoverning elite/higher masses.
- Saw society as an equilibrium similar to the models of the physical sciences.
