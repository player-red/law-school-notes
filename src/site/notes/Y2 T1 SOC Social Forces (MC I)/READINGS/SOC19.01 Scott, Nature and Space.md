In Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed, pp. 11-52.

- Commercialization of forests generally focuses on one product (such as timber) and ignores the multitude of uses trees have, as well as reduces biodiversity. 
- Plants assigned values are called "timber", "crops", etc. and those which have none are called "weeds" and "underbrush". The same logic is applied to fauna.
- Abstracting utilitarian logic used by the state is applied to other areas of life as well. 
- Regularity and neatness of forests allowed for greater efficiency in harvesting resources even by one untrained to do so, and promoted the organized aesthetic. 
- Ecosystems in a forest are far too complex to be recreated by administrators. Social patterns of human interaction are similarly complex.
	- Neither the state nor foresters have an interest in describing the entire reality as it is, and simplify the complexities of their areas of interest in order to manipulate them. 
- In order to control the taxation of citizens, the French government adopted a system of uniform measurements and maps.
	- Local practices were illegible to the state, creating a uniform system created legibility. 

Forcing the Tools of Legibility: Popular Measures, State Measures
- The variety of local practices became an impediment to administrative uniformity. 
- However, the local ways of measuring things were rooted in culture, tradition and practicality, something that could not be replicated by uniform forms of measurement.
The Politics of Measurement
- The act of measurement is a political act, affected by the competing interests of the groups of people using them. 
- Landlords often changed the unit of measurement in subtle ways to extract more resources from workers.
Statecraft and the Hieroglyphics of Measurement
- Illegibility led to compromise of state security: it was very difficult to track the status of food supply and markets when they all used different systems of measurement. 
- Equity could not be ensured in taxation without uniformity of measurement. 
Simplification and Standardization of Measurement
- Power to establish measures was a feudal prerogative, so resistance to standardization was high. 
- Factors that made standardization possible:
	- Growth of market exchange.
	- Popular sentiment + Enlightenment philosophy
	- Revolution and Napoleonic state-building
- The metric system became a means of not only administrative centralization and commercial reform, but also cultural progress.
- Homogenization of measurement also depended on homogenous citizenship: unless they were within the same legal sphere as equal citizens, they would have unequal rights with respect to measures.
	- Promotion of the idea of national citizenship.

Modernity: a state of life in which rationality and scientific thinking are given more importance.
Modernism: an ideology that applies principles of science and rationality to various aspects of life.
High modernism: using science and technology to reorder the social and natural world, mastering nature to meet human needs, making complex environments and concepts legible through ordering (often spatial ordering like grid planning) and a disregard for historical, geographical and social context of development.
