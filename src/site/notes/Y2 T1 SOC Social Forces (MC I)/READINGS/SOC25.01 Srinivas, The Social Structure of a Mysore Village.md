In India's Village, pp. 21-35.

- Sir Henry Maine brought the Indian village community into prominence and made it the basis of his theory of primitive communism of property.
	- Argued that land was owned jointly by kin-groups and individual ownership of land was later phenomenon in the history of history of human societies.
	- Based this theory on a type of north Indian village community.
	- Distinguished between two types of villages in India: joint and severalty.
	- Joint: North-West Frontier Province, Punjab, United Provinces.
		- Can be divided into pattidari (joint families have separate shares in cultivable land and hold waste + pasture in common) and zamindari (all land held and managed in common) subtypes.
		- Two classes of men: those with proprietary rights and those without.
	- Severalty/ryotwari: Peninsular and Central India, also in Bengal and Bihar before introduction of Permanent Settlement in 1793.
		- Each landholder is separately assessed and individually responsible for land revenue.
		- No waste land held in common, but common rights in use of waste.
- Common feature in all villages: panchayat.
- Villages functioned as little republics, generally very stable and self-sufficient. 
- Headman and account are key figures, usually a non-Brahmin and a Brahmin invariably respectively.
- Unity of the village:
	- Physical unity: in terms of harvest and disease.
	- Ritual unity: in order to protect themselves from the 'punishment of God'.
	- In relation to other people: hold pride in one's own village.
- Village = vertical unity of many castes.
- Caste = horizontal unity extending beyond the village.
- All castes in the village must cooperate for events, and all are interdependent. 
- Customary for one upper caste family and one family from the Untouchable caste to be linked together in a master-servant relationship, latter had responsibilities on ritual and social occasions. 
- Village is still self-sufficient, but more items produced outside are being brought in. 
	- Believes self-sufficient economy is only possible if the sub-castes adhere to their traditional occupations. 
- Calls inter-caste rivalry a natural corollary of caste solidarity, pride of one's own caste and contempt for the ways of other castes. 
- Caste courts can excommunicate people from their caste for offences such as an upper-caste living with a person from the Untouchable caste, etc.
- Village and caste authorities work together.
