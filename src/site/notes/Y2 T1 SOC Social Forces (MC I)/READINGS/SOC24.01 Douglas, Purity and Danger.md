Introduction and Chapter 1: Ritual Uncleanness, pp. 1-29. 

Introduction
- In the 19th century, people believed that two qualities separated 'primitive' religions from other 'great religions':
	- Fear
	- Defilement and hygiene
- Further study by anthropologists showed that these primitive cultures are not as fearful as they once believed, rather that they are happy and relaxed about god, some even viewing their god as a familiar friend.
- Dirt = disorder = something to be shunned = eliminating it is a positive effort to organize the environment.
- Pollution ideas work at two levels in society:
	- Instrumental: people trying to influence each other's behaviour, the laws of nature and power of the universe (+ social pressure) are used to sanction the beliefs of the people.
	- Expressive: pollution ideas used as analogies to describe beliefs about social order.
- Each society is a universe in and of itself, and subject to external pressure.
- Ideas of purity and impurity are subject to change despite seeming timeless and unchanging.
- Native of a culture may see themselves as a passive receiver of ideas of power and danger of this culture, but Douglas argues the opposite.

Ritual Uncleanness
- Dirt-avoidance rules can be set aside for friendship or charity.
- No actual connection between dirt and sacredness. 
- Sometimes dirt is used intentionally in order to show respect/sacredness: shows that these two categories are not mutually exclusive or opposites (sacred/profane, pure/impure).
- Old criterion for classifying religions as advanced/primitive: 
	- Primitive = holiness and uncleanness rules undistinguishable.
	- Advanced = rules of uncleanness disappeared from religion, became a matter of sanitation.
- Robertson Smith: believed that the modern civilized man represented a long process of evolution, and that customs which did not feed into the growing points of human history were irrational and primitive. 
	- Founded social anthropology by trying to find the common elements in the modern and primitive experience. 
- With the development of science, people stopped turning to religion for answers, and thus Green's philosophy was to develop morality as the essence of religion and turn away from revelation.
- Themes from Smith's lectures:
	- Exotic/mythological happenings and cosmological theories had little to do with religion. 
	- Religion rooted in ethical values of community life.
	- Believed Israel's religious life was fundamentally more ethical than those of the surrounding societies.
- Characteristics of Semitic religion:
	- Demonology (rousing fear)
	- Stable relationship with community god
- Israel rejected the demonology, focusing on the relationship with god as the true religion. 
- Magic: problem for anthropologists - a ritual not part of the cult of the community's god, and expected to have automatic effect. 
	- Hebrews and Protestants viewed it as meaningless nonsense that was useless without an interior experience of God.
- [[Y1 T1 SOC Society/PEOPLE/Émile Durkheim\|Émile Durkheim]]: primitive gods are part and parcel of the community and their form expresses the details of its structure + powers punishing and rewarding. 
	- Wanted to show that there was a requirement of common commitment to a common set of values/collective conscience.
	- Believed magic as beliefs and practices not operating within the communion of the church and hostile to it, but allowed that it was a form of primitive hygiene.
	- However, this undermines his own definition of religion: one that is of communal organization and separation of sacred from profane. 
	- Considers the sacred to be contagious, collective ideas projective outwards, expressions of morality that need to be hedged in with prohibitions. 
	- If sacred is contagious, how do you distinguish from non-sacred magic, which is also contagious?
- Frazer found magic was more than rules to avoid infection, since some rituals were to protect and procure benefits.
	- 3 stages of development of human culture: magic -> religion -> science.
	- Based on assumptions such as ethical refinement being a mark of advanced civilization, or that magic has nothing to do with morals or religion. 
	- Magic resulted from the early 'savage's' foolish inability to distinguish between his own subjective associations and external objective reality.
Class notes:
- Disagreed with the idea of sacred/profane, pure/impure as religious categories, rather saw them as the tendency to create social categories.
- Wanted to understand the classificatory tendencies of society.
- Humans positively reorder their environment to make it conform to an idea -> creating order and thus the structure of their society.
- Boundary maintenance between order and disorder has a general social logic.
- Rituals symbolize social organization/order and bring people together.
- Disagreed with the linear evolutionist view of religion.
